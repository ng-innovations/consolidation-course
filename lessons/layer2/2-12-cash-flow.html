Cash flow consolidation

The cash flow statement is one of the four primary financial statements, but it's the only one that can't simply be summed across subsidiaries. Cash flows reflect changes between two balance sheet snapshots — and at the group level, those snapshots have already been through translation, elimination, and ownership processing. Reconstructing meaningful cash flow categories from a transformed consolidated balance sheet requires its own logic.

Pipeline reference: Cash flow consolidation happens in Reporting (step D.12), after all balance sheet and income statement processing is complete.

The two methods — direct and indirect

Cash flow statements come in two presentation styles:

  • Direct method: Lists actual cash receipts and payments by category (cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers, etc.). More informative but rarely used in practice.
  • Indirect method: Starts with net income and adjusts for non-cash items and balance sheet movements to arrive at cash flow from operations. Standard practice in consolidated reporting.

This lesson focuses on the indirect method, which is what 95%+ of consolidated cash flow statements use.

The structure

A consolidated cash flow statement has three sections plus reconciliation lines:

SectionWhat's in it
Operating activitiesCash generated by the core business — sales, expenses, working capital changes. Starts with net income; adjusts for non-cash items (depreciation, etc.) and working capital movements (AR, AP, inventory).
Investing activitiesCash spent on or received from long-term assets — buying/selling property, equipment, acquisitions, investments.
Financing activitiesCash from/to capital providers — debt issuance/repayment, share issuance/buyback, dividends paid.
Effect of FX on cashA reconciling line specific to multi-currency groups — captures how translation rates affect reported cash balances.
Net change in cashSum of the four lines above; should equal closing cash minus opening cash.

Why you can't just sum subsidiary cash flows

Each subsidiary's local books produce a cash flow statement, but if you sum them across subs, several things go wrong at the group level:

  1. Intercompany cash flows. Cash moving between subs isn't a group cash flow — it's just internal movement. Sub A's "cash received from B" cancels Sub B's "cash paid to A." But the categorization may differ (Sub A might call it "from customers"; Sub B might call it "to suppliers"), so summing then eliminating gets messy.
  2. Translation effects. Local cash flows are in local currency. Translating those to group currency at average rates doesn't tie to the change in cash on the translated balance sheet — that change includes both transactional movements (at average rates) and the FX effect of revaluing opening cash balances at the new closing rate.
  3. Eliminations and topside entries. Consolidation adjustments don't appear in local cash flow statements but they do affect consolidated balance sheet movements.
  4. Acquisitions and divestitures. When a sub joins or leaves the group mid-period, its opening balance sheet either appears from nowhere or disappears — these need special treatment in the cash flow.

The standard approach — derive from consolidated movements

The reliable way to produce consolidated cash flow is to compute it from changes in consolidated balance sheet line items, with adjustments for known non-cash effects. This is sometimes called the "balance sheet difference" method.

The high-level approach:

  1. Take consolidated balance sheet at opening and closing.
  2. Compute the change in each line.
  3. Strip out non-cash effects (depreciation, FX revaluation, accruals for non-cash items, share-based comp).
  4. Categorize the remaining changes into operating, investing, or financing.
  5. Reconcile to net income at the top of operating activities.
  6. Compute the FX-on-cash reconciling line at the bottom.

The FX-on-cash line — what is it?

This line is unique to consolidation. Consider a simple example:

Sub A holds €100 of cash at year start. Opening FX rate: 1.10. So in group currency (USD), opening cash is $110. During the year, no cash flows at all — Sub A still holds €100. The closing FX rate has moved to 1.20. So in group currency, closing cash is $120.

On the consolidated balance sheet, cash went up by $10. But there's no cash flow — operations didn't generate $10, no investing or financing happened. The $10 increase is purely due to FX revaluation of the existing cash balance.

This is what the "effect of FX on cash" line captures. It's the difference between (closing cash − opening cash) and the sum of the three activity categories. Without this reconciling line, the cash flow statement wouldn't tie.

Acquisitions and divestitures in the cash flow

When a subsidiary is acquired mid-period, the group "absorbs" the sub's opening balance sheet (in the part of the period after acquisition). This shows up in two places on the cash flow:

  • In investing activities: "Cash paid for acquisition, net of cash acquired" — the actual cash outflow.
  • Implicit treatment of the rest of the acquired balance sheet: AR, AP, inventory, fixed assets, etc., come onto the consolidated balance sheet without those representing cash movements of the current group. They need to be excluded from the working-capital-change calculation that drives operating cash flow.

Divestitures are the mirror image. The dispositions disclosure typically details what was sold and the net cash effect.

Reconciliation — the tie-out

A consolidated cash flow statement is correct when:

Operating activities$X
+ Investing activities$Y
+ Financing activities$Z
+ Effect of FX on cash$F
Net change in cashX + Y + Z + F
+ Opening cash (from balance sheet)$Open
= Closing cash (from balance sheet)$Close

If the closing cash from the cash flow doesn't match the closing cash on the balance sheet — to the penny — something is wrong. This is the cash flow's tie-out point and is checked rigorously during close.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

Cash flow is the report most likely to break when consolidation infrastructure isn't designed for it. Considerations:

  • Every account needs a cash-flow category tag. Operating / investing / financing / cash. Without this metadata on the chart of accounts, you can't auto-categorize.
  • Non-cash adjustments need explicit identification. Depreciation, share-based comp, FX revaluation, accruals, deferred tax movements — all are non-cash flows that need to be backed out of the working-capital changes.
  • The FX-on-cash line must be computed from the difference, not from rates. Try to derive it independently and you'll get a different number every time. The reliable approach is plug = (closing cash − opening cash) − (sum of activity categories). The "plug" should be small and explainable if everything else is right.
  • Acquisitions need a dedicated workflow. The opening balance sheet of an acquired sub needs to be flagged and excluded from working-capital-change calculations.
  • Drill-down to the underlying movement is critical. Users will look at "Change in accounts receivable: $(2.5M)" and ask "where did this come from?" — they should be able to see the contributing entities and adjustments.
  • Sub-level cash flow statements are still useful internally. Even though the consolidated number is derived from balance sheet movements, having each sub's local-currency cash flow available for analysis is valuable.

Summary

  • The cash flow statement can't be produced by simply summing subsidiary cash flows — it has to be derived from movements in the consolidated balance sheet.
  • Indirect method is standard: starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items, categorizes movements into operating/investing/financing.
  • The "effect of FX on cash" reconciling line is unique to multi-currency consolidation — it absorbs FX revaluation of cash balances that doesn't represent real cash movement.
  • Acquisitions and divestitures need special treatment to avoid mistaking absorbed balance sheets for working-capital movements.
  • The closing cash on the cash flow statement must tie to the closing cash on the balance sheet, exactly. This is the cash flow's central reconciliation.
