Consolidated income statement

The consolidated income statement shows the group's revenue, expenses, and resulting profit over the period. It is the report that tells outside readers how the business performed. This lesson walks through what the consolidated version looks like and how each earlier pipeline step contributes to it.

Pipeline reference: The consolidated income statement is produced in Reporting (step D.12). It draws on intercompany elimination, unrealized-profit elimination, ownership allocation, and topside entries.

What the consolidated version looks like

The structure is the same as any company's income statement:

  • Revenue
  • Cost of goods sold
  • Gross profit
  • Operating expenses
  • Operating income
  • Other income/expense, interest, taxes
  • Net income

What's different at the group level:

  • Revenue is the group's external revenue only. Sales between subsidiaries are eliminated. If one sub sells $1M to another sub, and the buying sub re-sells to outside customers for $1.2M, the consolidated revenue is $1.2M, not $2.2M.
  • Expenses are similarly external only. Intercompany expenses are eliminated against the matching intercompany revenue.
  • Unrealized profit from intercompany sales is removed. If one sub made a profit selling to another sub and the goods are still sitting in the buyer's inventory at period end, that profit is reversed because it has not yet been earned from the outside world.
  • The bottom of the statement is split. Net income is shown as "Net income attributable to parent shareholders" plus "Net income attributable to non-controlling interest."

The rest of this lesson walks through how each of those differences is produced.

Example — a mocked-up consolidated income statement

Same example group as in Lesson 2.11: Acme Industrials (parent) owns 80% of BrightWave Energy (sub); both report in USD. Acme's consolidated income statement for the year looks like this:

Acme Industrials — consolidated income statement, year ended Dec 31Amount
Revenue$1,000
Cost of goods sold($550)
Gross profit$450
Operating expenses (including $50 depreciation)($200)
Net income$250
Attributable to parent shareholders$230
Attributable to non-controlling interest$20

Where these numbers reappear:

  • Net income $250 is the top line of the cash flow statement (Lesson 2.13) and the total of the two net-income rows on the statement of changes in equity (Lesson 2.14).
  • Attributable to parent $230 lands in the retained earnings column of the equity statement.
  • Attributable to NCI $20 lands in the NCI column of the equity statement.
  • After the equity statement also absorbs dividends, its closing balances become the equity lines on the balance sheet (Lesson 2.11).

A worked example

The parent owns 80% of a single subsidiary. Both report in USD. The standalone income statements are:

Revenue from external customers$900$400
Revenue from Sub (intercompany)$100—
Total revenue$1,000$400
Cost of goods sold($600)($200)
of which IC purchase from Parent (all resold)—($100)
Operating expenses($100)($100)
Net income$300$100

Sub bought $100 of goods from Parent during the year and resold all of them externally. There is no leftover IC inventory at year-end, so no unrealized profit to worry about in this case.

Step 1 — Combine

Naive sum gives revenue of $1,400, total expenses of $1,000, and net income of $400.

Step 2 — Eliminate intercompany revenue and expense

The $100 IC sale appears as revenue on Parent's books and as part of cost on Sub's books. Both lines are removed. Revenue drops to $1,300, COGS drops to $700. Net income unchanged at $400. See Lesson 2.6.

Step 3 — Split net income between parent and NCI

Of the $400 net income, $300 came from Parent (100% attributable to parent shareholders) and $100 came from Sub. 80% of Sub's $100 ($80) belongs to parent shareholders; 20% ($20) belongs to NCI. See Lesson 2.8.

Step 4 — Consolidated income statement

LineConsolidated
Revenue$1,300

Gross profit$600
Operating expenses($200)

Attributable to parent shareholders$380
Attributable to non-controlling interest$20

The two attribution lines are required presentation under both US GAAP and IFRS. They are what tells parent shareholders which portion of group profit is theirs.

What changes if Sub still holds IC inventory

Suppose Sub had bought $100 from Parent but only resold $70 by year-end, with $30 still in inventory. Parent's margin on the IC sale was 25% — so $30 of Sub's inventory contains $7.50 of Parent's unrecognized profit. The consolidation reverses that $7.50: Sub's inventory comes down by $7.50, and COGS goes up by $7.50 (or revenue is reduced, depending on convention). Group net income falls by $7.50 because the group has not yet earned that profit from the outside world. See Lesson 2.9.

Why each consolidation step shows up here

Intercompany revenue and expense both go to zero

The group views itself as a single entity. A sale from one of its pockets to another is not a sale at all from outside-the-fence. Eliminating IC revenue without eliminating the matching cost would understate consolidated profit; eliminating both keeps the income statement honest.

Unrealized profit on IC inventory is reversed

Profit on an internal sale is not real profit until the goods leave the group. Until then, the profit sits in the buyer's inventory account and inflates both group assets and group earnings. The elimination reduces inventory back to original cost and reverses the corresponding profit.

Net income splits at the bottom

The whole P&L above the net income line represents activity of the group as a single economic unit, including the sub's full revenue and expense. But ownership is not always 100%. The split tells parent shareholders how much of the bottom line is actually theirs, and how much is owed to outside (NCI) shareholders. See Lesson 2.8.

OCI and CTA flow through a separate section

Certain gains and losses — currency translation differences, some pension remeasurements, certain hedge effects — bypass net income and go directly to other comprehensive income. Consolidated income statements often include a separate "Other comprehensive income" section below net income, or are presented as two statements (income statement plus a statement of comprehensive income). Translation-related OCI feeds the CTA line in equity. See Lesson 2.4.

The tie-out checks

  • Net income flows to retained earnings on the balance sheet. Parent's share lands in parent equity's retained earnings; NCI's share lands in the NCI column. Both should reconcile through the statement of changes in equity (Lesson 2.14).
  • Net income also drives the top line of operating cash flow. Under the indirect method, the cash flow statement starts with net income and adjusts for non-cash items. The number must match. See Lesson 2.13.
  • NCI's share of net income equals NCI ownership % times the sub's net income. If it doesn't, an unrealized-profit reversal or a topside has been allocated incorrectly.
  • OCI on the income statement equals OCI activity on the statement of changes in equity. They are two views of the same numbers.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

The income statement looks like a simple aggregation, but it depends on several pieces of metadata that have to be in place upstream:

  • Every revenue and expense transaction needs a counterparty tag. Without it, you can't tell which dollars are intercompany and which are external. This belongs on the line item, not as a separate reconciliation step.
  • Unrealized profit needs an inventory-aging join. The engine needs to know what part of intercompany inventory is still on hand at period end and at what margin it was sold internally. This is harder than it looks at scale.
  • NCI attribution is a derived calculation, not stored data. Compute it from current ownership and current sub net income; never persist parent-share / NCI-share as separate fields that can drift out of sync.
  • Comparative columns are mandatory. Current period, prior period, year-to-date, year-to-date prior — most income statement views show four or more columns. Plan for it.
  • Drill-down on revenue should reach the underlying transactions. A user clicking the revenue line should see contributing subs, then individual customers, then individual invoices if needed. The most common audit question is "where did this come from?"

Summary

  • The consolidated income statement shows the group's external revenue, external costs, and resulting profit.
  • Intercompany revenue and expense both cancel; unrealized profit on inventory still held internally is reversed.
  • The bottom of the statement splits net income between parent shareholders and non-controlling interest.
  • OCI and CTA sit in a separate section (or separate statement) below net income.
  • Net income ties forward to retained earnings on the balance sheet and to the top of the cash flow statement.
