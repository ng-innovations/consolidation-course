Non-controlling interest

When a parent controls a subsidiary but doesn't own 100% of it, the remaining ownership belongs to outside parties. Consolidation still pulls in 100% of the subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses (because the parent controls them) — but it has to separately account for the outsiders' share of equity and earnings. That outsider share is the non-controlling interest.

Pipeline reference: NCI on opening equity is established as part of Consolidation of Investments (step C.10). The NCI share of current period income and OCI is allocated in Group Adjustments (step C.11).

The core idea

Suppose Parent P owns 80% of Sub S. The other 20% is held by outside shareholders. Consolidation accounting says:

  • Bring in 100% of S's assets and liabilities (full consolidation — P controls them).
  • Bring in 100% of S's revenue and expenses (same reason).
  • On the equity side of the balance sheet, split S's equity between P (80%) and NCI (20%).
  • At the bottom of the income statement, allocate S's net income between P (80%) and NCI (20%).

So the consolidated balance sheet ends up with two equity sections:

Equity attributable to parent shareholders$P's share
Non-controlling interest$NCI's share
Total equityCombined

And the consolidated income statement ends with two lines:

Net income$Total

Attributable to non-controlling interest$NCI's share

A worked example

Parent P owns 80% of Sub S, acquired at the beginning of the year for $800. S's equity at acquisition was $800 (no goodwill on this acquisition; this is a clean book-value purchase to keep the example simple). During the year, S earns $100 of net income.

At year end:

S's balance sheet at year end
Net assets$900
Equity$900
Common stock$100
Retained earnings (opening $700 + $100 income)$800

The consolidation needs to:

  1. Eliminate P's investment in S ($800) against S's opening equity ($800). Since P only owns 80%, only 80% of opening equity is "P's." The other 20% (worth $160 at opening) becomes opening NCI.
  2. Allocate the year's $100 of income: 80% ($80) to P, 20% ($20) to NCI.

End-of-period NCI on balance sheet: $160 (opening) + $20 (current income) = $180. That equals 20% × $900 (S's year-end equity), which is the sanity check.

Full goodwill vs. partial goodwill

The example above ignored goodwill. When the price paid implies goodwill, you have to decide how to measure NCI:

Partial goodwillFull goodwill
What's recognized Goodwill = price paid by parent − parent's share of net assets Goodwill = total implied enterprise value − total net assets
NCI measured at Proportionate share of identifiable net assets only Fair value of the NCI's stake (includes their implied share of goodwill)
Required by Optional under IFRS Required under US GAAP; optional under IFRS

Example: P pays $800 for 80% of S, where S has net assets of $800 (so the implied 100% value is $1,000).

  • Partial goodwill: Goodwill = $800 paid − (80% × $800) = $800 − $640 = $160. NCI = 20% × $800 = $160.
  • Full goodwill: Implied total value = $1,000. Total goodwill = $1,000 − $800 = $200. NCI = $200 (their stake at full implied value).

In the full-goodwill case, both the goodwill on the balance sheet and the NCI are larger by $40 (the NCI's implied share of goodwill).

NCI in OCI and dividends

Other comprehensive income from a partly-owned sub is also split. If S has $50 of OCI in the period, $40 goes to parent equity, $10 to NCI.

Dividends paid by S to its outside shareholders reduce NCI (the outsiders are taking their share home). Dividends paid by S to P are intercompany and get eliminated entirely — they don't reduce NCI.

Changes in ownership without losing control

If P buys an additional 10% of S (going from 80% to 90%) without losing or gaining control (it already had control), the transaction is treated as an equity transaction between owners — not as a new acquisition. No new goodwill is recognized. NCI decreases by 10% of S's equity, and any difference between what P paid and that NCI reduction goes directly to parent equity (not through the income statement).

If P sells some of its stake but still keeps control, the same equity-transaction treatment applies in reverse.

If P sells enough to lose control, the situation becomes a divestiture — S is no longer a subsidiary, and the remaining stake (if any) is reclassified to associate (equity method) or financial asset. That's its own area and tends to be infrequent.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

NCI calculations sit at the heart of the consolidation engine. Design considerations:

  • Store ownership percentages versioned over time. Acquisitions, step-ups, partial sales — all change ownership. The pipeline needs to know the ownership % effective for each period.
  • Compute NCI on every line of equity and every line of net income. It's not just one number — it's a parallel allocation across many GLs.
  • Surface NCI in reports as a column or section. Stakeholders want to see "of this $X, how much is parent's, how much is NCI's."
  • Method choice (full vs. partial goodwill) is per-acquisition and permanent. Lock it in at acquisition time; don't let users flip it later.
  • Changes-in-ownership-without-loss-of-control should be a distinct transaction type. It looks like an investment change but doesn't generate goodwill — different journal entry pattern.

Summary

  • NCI is the slice of a subsidiary's equity owned by parties outside the group; consolidation still includes 100% of the sub's assets/liabilities/revenue/expenses but separately tracks the NCI portion of equity and income.
  • NCI shows up in two places: as a section of consolidated equity on the balance sheet, and as an allocation of net income at the bottom of the income statement.
  • The two measurement methods (full vs. partial goodwill) affect how much goodwill and how much NCI are recognized, but not the operating results.
  • OCI and dividends are also allocated proportionally.
  • Ownership changes that don't affect control are equity transactions — not new acquisitions.
