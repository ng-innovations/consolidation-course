Layer 2 — Lesson 2

Trial balance collection & validation

Every consolidation cycle starts with the same task: collect a trial balance from every subsidiary, and verify that what arrived is usable.

Pipeline reference: Trial balance collection and validation runs at ingest. After it passes, the subsidiary's data lands in Local state, ready for the rest of the pipeline.

How TBs arrive

Subsidiaries submit their TBs in different ways depending on the customer's setup:

  • Direct integration. The system pulls TBs from the subsidiary's accounting software automatically — NetSuite, SAP, Dynamics, QuickBooks, etc. The cleanest setup.
  • File upload. The subsidiary's controller exports a TB from their system to CSV or Excel and uploads it. The format usually has to match a defined template.
  • Web entry. For very small subsidiaries or non-financial entities, the data is sometimes keyed in directly.

Whatever the source, what arrives is normalized into a common internal format before validation can run.

How intercompany counterparty is identified

Identifying the account, period, amount, and currency on a TB line is usually mechanical — that information falls out of the structure of the data itself. Identifying the intercompany counterparty is the part that varies, because subs encode it in their books in one of two ways. From the consolidation engine's perspective either is workable; the choice usually comes down to what each sub's source accounting system can produce.

Approach A: account-encoded

Have a separate account for each trading partner. The account code is the counterparty identifier. The engine can read the account and know who's on the other side.

Approach B: dimensional

Have one IC AR account and one IC AP account, and attach a "trading partner" tag (a dimension) to each line. The account stays single; the partner identification moves to a separate field on each line.

What drives which approach a sub uses is mostly the capability of its source accounting system. Smaller companies running simpler systems (QuickBooks and the like) tend toward Approach A — separate accounts — because that's what's natively exportable. Larger entities on full ERPs (NetSuite, SAP, Oracle, Workday) typically support dimensions natively and lean toward Approach B.

The ingest layer needs to accept either encoding and normalize them into a single internal representation, so downstream steps (matching, elimination) don't have to care which approach the sub used.

The role of dimensions

A trial balance line doesn't have to be one row per account. Modern systems usually capture additional segments on each row:

The dimensions become available for slicing the data in reports later. They also drive intercompany matching: the counterparty on Sub A's row tells the system "this $80,000 of revenue should match against Sub B's books."

What validation checks

Once a TB is in the system, validation has to verify it's usable. The checks fall into three buckets.

Structural validation

  • Every row has a valid account code (exists in the local COA).
  • Every account code has a mapping to a group account, or routes to Suspense.
  • Required fields are present (amount, currency, period).
  • Amounts are numeric and within sane bounds.
  • The currency code matches the entity's expected functional currency.

Mathematical validation

  • The TB balances. Signed amounts sum to zero.
  • Opening balances tie to last period's closing balances. Each balance sheet account's opening balance should equal what the same account had at the prior period close. See Lesson 2.3.
  • Sign conventions. Asset and expense accounts should have debit balances; liability, equity, and revenue accounts should have credit balances. Unusual signs should at least raise a warning.

Business validation

  • Every expected subsidiary has submitted for the period.
  • Materially unusual variances from prior period are flagged for review.
  • Subsidiary-level intercompany totals agree to its counterparty's books within a tolerance.

What happens on validation failure

The system decides what to do based on severity:

SeverityBehavior
Critical (TB doesn't balance, no mapping at all)Reject the submission. Block this entity from progressing.
Error (unmapped account, missing required field)Accept but route problematic items to Suspense and surface them for resolution. Block period close until resolved.
Warning (unusual sign, large variance)Accept and flag for review. Don't block close, but record acknowledgment.

The principle: never let bad data flow silently through the pipeline. Every issue either gets resolved or gets explicitly acknowledged.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

Ingest is the boundary between "the world's mess" and "our clean pipeline." Everything downstream assumes well-formed data, so this layer carries a lot of weight.

  • Fail loudly, fail early. A validation error caught at ingest is cheap; the same error caught at reporting is expensive.
  • Capture rejections, don't drop them. Bad rows should be visible and resolvable, not silently discarded.
  • Make resubmission cheap. Subsidiaries resubmit TBs many times during a typical close. Each resubmission needs to re-run validation cleanly and update workflow state.

Summary

  • Each period, every subsidiary submits a trial balance in its functional currency — via integration, file upload, or web entry.
  • Internally, TB lines are normalized: entity, period, group account, signed amount, currency, optional counterparty.
  • Intercompany counterparty is encoded either as a dedicated account or as a dimension; the ingest layer accepts both.
  • Validation checks fall into three buckets (structural, mathematical, business) and three severities (critical, error, warning).
  • The close can't progress until every expected submission reaches Approved.
