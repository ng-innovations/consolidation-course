Ownership & consolidation methods

The ownership relationships between entities in a group drive how each subsidiary is consolidated. This lesson covers how ownership is structured, how the appropriate consolidation method gets selected for each entity, and how indirect ownership chains are resolved.

Pipeline reference: The ownership structure is configured in step A.2 (Entity Setups) and then used by steps C.10 (Investment Elimination) and C.11 (NCI Allocation).

What "ownership" means here

For consolidation purposes, "ownership" is the percentage of voting equity the parent (or an intermediate group entity) holds in the subsidiary. It determines:

  • Whether the entity is consolidated at all
  • Which consolidation method applies (full, equity, or fair value)
  • For fully consolidated subsidiaries, the split between parent equity and NCI

Ownership percentages can change over time — through acquisitions, divestitures, additional share issuances, buybacks, or step acquisitions. The system needs to track ownership history, not just the current percentage.

Picking the consolidation method

From Lesson 1.3, recall the three main methods:

MethodUsed whenEffect on consolidated statements
Full consolidationParent has control (usually > 50%)100% of subsidiary's lines added; NCI tracks the unowned portion
Equity methodSignificant influence (usually 20–50%) or joint controlSingle line on the balance sheet ("Investment in associate"); single line on income statement (share of profit)
Cost / fair valueNo significant influence (usually < 20%)Investment at cost or market value; dividends as income only

The thresholds (20%, 50%) are guidelines. The actual test is whether the parent has control, significant influence, or neither. A 45% ownership might confer control if it comes with board appointment rights; a 60% ownership might not confer control if shareholder agreements require unanimity for major decisions.

So in practice, each entity has its consolidation method explicitly assigned in setup, not derived purely from ownership percentage.

Direct vs. indirect ownership

Few real-world groups are flat. Most have intermediate holding companies between the ultimate parent and the operating subsidiaries.

Computing effective ownership

For a subsidiary that's owned indirectly through one or more holding companies, the effective ownership is the product of the ownership percentages along the chain.

Atlas Tools: Owned 80% by Maple Holdings. Maple Holdings is 100% owned by the parent. So:
Effective ownership = 100% × 80% = 80%

Sapphire Media: Owned 100% by Granite Holdings. Granite Holdings is 75% owned by the parent. So:
Effective ownership = 75% × 100% = 75%

NCI for Atlas Tools is then 20% (the share that doesn't roll up to the ultimate parent). NCI for Sapphire Media is 25%.

Why this works mathematically

Suppose Sapphire Media's net income is $100. Granite Holdings owns 100% of it, so all $100 belongs to Granite Holdings. But the ultimate parent only owns 75% of Granite Holdings — so only 75% of Granite Holdings' piece of Sapphire Media (75% × $100 = $75) belongs to the ultimate parent. The remaining $25 belongs to Granite Holdings' non-controlling shareholders.

The product-of-percentages formula gives the right answer because at each step, the unowned portion stays with the previous level.

Multiple paths to the same entity

Sometimes a group has more than one ownership path to a single entity — for example, when the parent owns shares directly and additional shares through a holding company. Effective ownership is then the sum of the effective ownerships along each path.

Ownership change during a period

If ownership changed mid-period — for instance, the parent acquired an additional 10% in March of a December year-end — consolidation has to handle this carefully.

The most common rule: the income statement is split based on when ownership applied. The first three months use the pre-acquisition ownership; the remaining nine months use the post-acquisition ownership. The balance sheet at period end uses the final ownership.

For new acquisitions, the acquired entity's results are only consolidated from the acquisition date forward — not retroactively for the full period.

Step acquisitions

A particularly complex case is the "step acquisition" — when an existing equity-method investment becomes a controlled subsidiary via additional purchase.

Example: Parent owns 30% of Target (accounted for under equity method). Parent buys an additional 25%, taking ownership to 55%. Target is now a subsidiary. The accounting requires:

  1. Re-measure the original 30% investment to fair value.
  2. Recognize any gain or loss on the remeasurement in net income.
  3. Apply purchase accounting to the whole 55%, calculating goodwill as if the entire stake had been acquired at fair value.
  4. Begin full consolidation from the acquisition date forward.

Step acquisitions are infrequent but require careful handling. Most consolidation systems treat them as a separate workflow.

Divestitures and de-consolidation

When the parent sells enough of a subsidiary to lose control, that entity stops being fully consolidated. The retained interest (if any) becomes an equity-method or fair-value investment.

The transition is essentially the reverse of a step acquisition — calculate the gain or loss on the sale, recognize it in income, and switch the accounting method going forward.

Joint ventures and associates

Entities accounted for under the equity method aren't combined line-by-line. Instead, the group recognizes:

  • On the balance sheet: a single asset, "Investment in associate" (or "Investment in joint venture"), starting at cost and adjusted each period for the group's share of the associate's net income, less dividends received.
  • On the income statement: a single line, "Share of profit of associate," equal to the ownership % times the associate's net income for the period.

The associate's revenue, expenses, assets, and liabilities are not blended into the group's totals.

State transition

Ownership configuration itself is part of setup (state-independent). The actual application happens in the investment elimination step (C.10) and NCI allocation step (C.11), which transition data from Eliminated state to Ownership-Adjusted state.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

The entity model is the foundation everything ownership-related rests on. Some key requirements:

  • Ownership relationships are edges in a directed graph. Effective ownership is computed by walking from the ultimate parent down to each entity through all paths.
  • Each ownership edge is effective-dated. The graph "as of" a given date may differ from "today's" graph.
  • Each entity has a consolidation method per period. An entity can be equity-method this year and full-consolidation next year (after an acquisition that pushed ownership over the control threshold).
  • Compute effective ownership programmatically, not by hand. For deep or wide ownership graphs, even careful humans get the math wrong.
  • Acquisition and divestiture events should be modeled as first-class transactions, not just edits to the ownership table. They carry accounting consequences (gains, losses, goodwill changes) that need their own audit trail.

Summary

  • Ownership percentages determine consolidation method: full (control), equity (significant influence), or cost/fair value (no influence).
  • Effective ownership in a multi-level group is the product of percentages along the chain.
  • Multiple ownership paths to the same entity are summed.
  • Ownership changes within a period are pro-rated; acquired entities are consolidated only from the acquisition date.
  • Step acquisitions and divestitures are separate workflows that involve remeasurement at the transition point.
  • Associates and joint ventures are not combined line-by-line; only a single equity-method line appears in consolidated statements.
