Consolidated balance sheet

The consolidated balance sheet is a snapshot of what the group owns and owes at the end of the period. It is the first of the four primary financial statements and it is what every other report ties back to. This lesson walks through what the consolidated version looks like and how every earlier step of the pipeline contributes to it.

Pipeline reference: The consolidated balance sheet is produced in Reporting (step D.12). It draws on outputs of every preceding consolidation step.

What the consolidated version looks like

The structure is the same as any company's balance sheet:

  • Assets — current and non-current
  • Liabilities — current and non-current
  • Equity

What's different at the group level:

  • The numbers represent the whole group — combined cash from every subsidiary, combined receivables, combined property, etc.
  • Intercompany balances are gone. If one sub owes another sub $100, neither the receivable nor the payable appears in the consolidated balance sheet. They have been eliminated.
  • The parent's "Investment in Subsidiaries" line is gone. It was eliminated against the subsidiaries' equity.
  • Equity has a non-controlling interest line — the portion of subsidiary equity belonging to outside shareholders.
  • Goodwill may appear as an asset — from past acquisitions where the parent paid more than book value.
  • A CTA line sits within equity if there are foreign subsidiaries.

Each of these is the visible result of an earlier pipeline step. The rest of this lesson walks through the build-up.

Example — a mocked-up consolidated balance sheet

To make the rest of this lesson concrete, here is a small example group: Acme Industrials (parent) owns 80% of BrightWave Energy (sub); both report in USD. The same example is used in Lesson 2.12, Lesson 2.13, and Lesson 2.14, so the four primary reports tie together.

Three things in this report show up directly in other statements:

  • Common stock $300, Retained earnings $850, and NCI $140 are each the closing balance of the matching column on the statement of changes in equity (Lesson 2.14).

A worked example

Parent owns 80% of Sub. Sub owes Parent $100. Both report in USD, so no translation. Their separate balance sheets at year-end look like this:

LineParentSub
Cash$500$300
Intercompany receivable (from Sub)$100—
Other assets$2,400$900
Investment in Sub$1,000—
Total assets$4,000$1,200
Intercompany payable (to Parent)—$100
Other liabilities$1,500$100
Common stock$500$200
Retained earnings$2,000$800
Total liabilities + equity$4,000$1,200

Two extra facts: Parent acquired Sub when Sub's equity was $900 (so $100 of goodwill exists from that acquisition, using the parent's-share method); and the IC receivable and payable agree.

Step 1 — Combine

Stack Parent and Sub line by line. Total assets = $4,000 + $1,200 = $5,200. But this is still wrong — the $100 IC balance shows up on both sides, and Investment in Sub is still sitting there alongside Sub's own equity.

Step 2 — Eliminate intercompany

Remove the $100 receivable on Parent's books against the $100 payable on Sub's books. Both lines go to zero. Total assets drop to $5,100; liabilities also drop. No effect on equity. See Lesson 2.6 for the mechanics.

Step 3 — Eliminate the investment

Parent's $1,000 Investment in Sub is cancelled against Sub's equity ($1,000 = $200 common stock + $800 retained earnings). The $100 of goodwill recognized at acquisition is added as a new asset. See Lesson 2.7.

Step 4 — Apply NCI

20% of Sub's equity belongs to outside shareholders. After investment elimination, Sub's remaining equity (the post-acquisition movement) is $0 at the snapshot above, but in general the NCI line carries 20% of Sub's net assets. Here, NCI is 20% of $1,000 = $200, presented as a separate line in equity. The parent's share of Sub's equity has already been absorbed by the investment elimination. See Lesson 2.8.

Step 5 — Consolidated balance sheet

Note that the IC lines are gone, Investment in Sub is gone, goodwill is on its own line, and NCI sits in equity. The parent's retained earnings of $1,900 reflects the $100 of post-acquisition profit attributable to the parent that Sub had earned ($800 retained earnings minus $700 at acquisition = $100, of which 80% = $80 goes to the parent; the example numbers are rounded for illustration).

Why each consolidation step shows up here

Intercompany balances cancel

From the group's outside-the-fence view, one sub owing another is the same as money in one pocket and a note in another. It is not a real receivable or payable. Lesson 2.6 covers the matching and elimination mechanics.

Investment in Subs is replaced by the sub's net assets

On its own books, the parent carries a single asset called Investment in Sub. After consolidation, that line is gone and what replaces it is the sub's actual assets, minus its actual liabilities, plus any goodwill from acquisition. The investment line and the sub's equity cancel each other out. See Lesson 2.7.

NCI sits inside equity

For partly-owned subs, the share of equity attributable to outside shareholders is presented on its own line within equity — not in liabilities and not in parent equity. Both US GAAP and IFRS require this. See Lesson 2.8.

CTA appears within equity if foreign subs exist

When subs in foreign currencies are translated, balance sheet items at one rate and income items at another inevitably produce a small imbalance. That imbalance is parked in equity as cumulative translation adjustment. See Lesson 2.4.

Goodwill stays on the balance sheet

The excess of acquisition price over net assets is goodwill. Once recognized, it lives on the consolidated balance sheet as an indefinite-life asset and is tested for impairment each period. It is one of the few balance sheet lines that exists only at the consolidated level — no individual subsidiary carries the group's goodwill.

The tie-out checks

A consolidated balance sheet is correct when:

  • Assets equal liabilities plus equity. The fundamental accounting identity. If it does not balance, the elimination or NCI math has gone wrong somewhere.
  • Opening balances tie to the prior period's closing. Every line carried forward; the carryforward tie is non-negotiable.
  • Cash on the balance sheet matches the closing cash on the consolidated cash flow statement. See Lesson 2.13.
  • Total equity matches the closing balance on the statement of changes in equity. See Lesson 2.14.
  • Retained earnings equals prior closing plus the period's net income minus dividends. This links the income statement to the balance sheet through the equity statement.

These are the cross-statement consistency checks auditors look at first.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

The balance sheet is the easiest report to render once the underlying data states are correct — it is just a presentation of the Top data. But that "once they are correct" is the entire pipeline. A few practical points:

  • Render from a single data state, not a snowflake of joins. By the time the balance sheet is presented, all transformations should have already happened. Avoid running elimination logic in the report layer.
  • Every line needs drill-down to the contributing entities. A user clicking "Cash $800" should see Parent's $500 and Sub's $300. Clicking "Goodwill $100" should reveal which acquisition it came from.
  • The NCI line is a query, not a stored field. NCI changes period to period based on subsidiary equity movements. Compute it from current ownership and current sub equity; don't try to store it as a separate balance.
  • Comparative columns are mandatory. A balance sheet almost always shows two periods side by side (current vs. prior year-end). Build for it from day one.
  • Currency view-switching matters. Users often want to see the same balance sheet in USD and in EUR — same data, different presentation rates. Translation should be a view layer, not a re-run of the pipeline.

Summary

  • The consolidated balance sheet is the group's snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity at period end.
  • It looks like any company's balance sheet, but with intercompany balances eliminated, the Investment in Subs line replaced by the sub's net assets plus goodwill, NCI sitting inside equity, and CTA recorded when foreign subs are present.
  • Producing it is mostly a matter of pulling the Top data state and presenting it — the heavy work happens earlier in the pipeline.
  • Tie-outs across the four primary statements (cash, equity, retained earnings) are the consistency checks auditors and controllers run first.
