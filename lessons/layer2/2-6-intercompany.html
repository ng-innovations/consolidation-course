Intercompany eliminations

When companies within a group transact with each other, those transactions appear on both companies' books as if they were external. From the group's perspective, they're internal — the group is moving money between its own pockets. Intercompany elimination is the process of removing these internal transactions from the consolidated statements.

Pipeline reference: Step C.9, IC Elimination. After this step, data moves from Standardized state to Eliminated state.

Why eliminate?

Consider a simple example. Sub A sells $100 of services to Sub B.

Sub A's booksDRCR
AR — intercompany (Sub B)100
Revenue — intercompany100
Sub B's booksDRCR
Operating expense — intercompany100
AP — intercompany (Sub A)100

If we just added these together for the group view, we'd see:

  • $100 of revenue (from Sub A)
  • $100 of expense (from Sub B)
  • $100 of AR (Sub A's receivable)
  • $100 of AP (Sub B's payable)

None of this is real from the group's perspective. The group didn't earn $100 — it just moved money between two of its own divisions. No external counterparty was involved. The receivable and payable cancel because the group can't owe money to itself.

So we eliminate all four lines:

Elimination entryDRCR
Revenue — intercompany100
Operating expense — intercompany100
AP — intercompany100
AR — intercompany100

Now the consolidated view correctly shows the group's external activity only.

The four common elimination categories

1. AR vs. AP

If Sub A is owed money by Sub B, Sub A has intercompany AR and Sub B has intercompany AP. The two cancel exactly (when correctly recorded) and both get eliminated.

2. Intercompany revenue vs. intercompany expense

When one sub sells to another, one has intercompany revenue and the other has intercompany expense. These offset and both get eliminated.

3. Intercompany loans

One sub lends money to another. One has a loan receivable; the other has a loan payable. Plus any associated interest income/expense and accrued interest.

4. Intercompany dividends

A sub pays a dividend to the parent. The parent records dividend income; the sub reduces equity. From the group's perspective, the parent didn't receive income — the group just transferred cash internally. The dividend income is eliminated; the sub's equity reduction stays (because dividends represent a real distribution to shareholders, but for the group, only the dividends to external shareholders count — see lesson 2.8 on NCI).

Matching

The mechanics of elimination rest on matching: identifying pairs of intercompany balances and confirming they agree.

A match requires several things to align:

  • The two entities reference each other as counterparties.
  • The amounts agree (after currency translation, if applicable).
  • The accounts are paired (e.g., AR on one side matches AP on the other; revenue matches expense).
  • The period is the same.

Mismatches

Intercompany mismatches are very common, and one of the biggest time-eaters in a typical close. Causes:

  • Timing. Sub A booked the sale on March 31; Sub B didn't receive the invoice until April 2. Sub A shows revenue and AR in Q1; Sub B shows nothing yet.
  • Recording errors. One side coded the transaction to the wrong counterparty entity, or omitted the IC flag.
  • Disputes. Sub A says Sub B owes $100; Sub B says only $90 because of a quantity dispute or service issue.
  • FX rate differences. Each side may have used different rates to translate the transaction into their local books before the consolidation translation step.
  • Cut-off. A shipment in transit at period end — the seller may have already recognized revenue and the receivable; the buyer may not have recorded the purchase yet.

Tolerances

Small mismatches are usually accepted as immaterial and absorbed into an "intercompany variance" account. The system typically allows a configurable tolerance per type of mismatch:

Mismatch sizeAction
< $100 (or < 0.1%)Auto-absorb into variance account
$100 – $10,000Flag for review; eliminate at smaller side; absorb difference
> $10,000Block elimination; require resolution before close

The exact thresholds depend on the group's materiality policy.

Mechanical elimination (no counterparty data)

When subsidiaries don't tag transactions with counterparty entity IDs, the system can't match pairwise. Some groups fall back to a different approach: just eliminate everything in dedicated intercompany accounts, regardless of which counterparty.

This works only if every sub's intercompany account totals sum to roughly zero across the group (because what's a receivable for one is a payable for another). It's coarser, less auditable, and harder to investigate when something's off — but it's sometimes the only practical option.

Currency aspects

Intercompany matching gets tricky when the two sides are in different currencies. The transaction enters Sub A's books in EUR and Sub B's books in USD. After currency translation, both are in the group currency — but the exact translated values may differ based on which rate type applies to each account on each entity.

Common policy: match in the group currency after translation, applying tolerances generous enough to absorb minor FX rounding differences.

The mechanics of elimination as journal entries

Elimination is implemented as journal entries posted at the consolidation level — these don't go back to the subsidiaries' books. They sit at a separate consolidation-only layer.

From a pipeline standpoint, after elimination entries are posted:

  • The original IC revenue and IC expense still exist on each entity's books — but the elimination entry offsets them so they don't appear in the consolidated income statement.
  • Same for IC AR and IC AP.
  • The system can still report on the original IC activity (e.g., for the intercompany matrix) — it just doesn't end up in external statements.

The intercompany matrix

One of the most useful reports a consolidation system produces is the IC matrix — a grid showing all intercompany balances between every pair of entities, with mismatches highlighted.

The matrix is symmetric in the abstract — if Sub A says it's owed $80 from Sub B, then Sub B should say it owes $80 to Sub A. When the two sides disagree, the matrix is the place where the discrepancy gets surfaced and resolved.

State transition

After elimination runs, data moves from Standardized to Eliminated. At this point:

  • Internal transactions between group entities have been removed.
  • The combined entity-level data, when added together, reflects only the group's external activity (with two exceptions that will be handled in later steps: investment in subsidiaries hasn't been eliminated yet, and ownership math hasn't been applied).
  • Intercompany variance accounts reflect any unmatched residuals.

Unrealized profit — a special case

One particular kind of intercompany activity gets its own treatment: profit on intercompany inventory transfers when the inventory hasn't been sold to an external customer yet. This is the unrealized profit elimination, covered in Lesson 2.9.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

Intercompany matching is the most operationally painful part of every close. The product can save customers a tremendous amount of time here. Some design principles:

  • Counterparty entity is required for IC-flagged accounts. Validate at ingest, not at elimination.
  • Tolerances are configurable per account type / counterparty pair. Different relationships have different reasonable mismatch thresholds.
  • Surface mismatches as work items, not just report rows. The user should be able to click into a mismatch and see both sides, attempt resolution, and track progress.
  • Eliminations are journal entries, posted in a layer of their own. They sit on top of subsidiary data without modifying it — so we can drill back to "this $100 was eliminated by the consolidation, but Sub A's local books still show $100 revenue."
  • Make the IC matrix a primary report. It's the first place users go when something doesn't reconcile.

Summary

  • Intercompany transactions between group entities have to be removed in consolidation — they don't represent external activity.
  • The four common categories: AR vs. AP, IC revenue vs. IC expense, IC loans, IC dividends.
  • Matching identifies pairs of intercompany balances; both sides must agree (within tolerance).
  • Mismatches are common — caused by timing, errors, disputes, FX, or cut-off — and need resolution or variance absorption.
  • Eliminations are posted as journal entries at the consolidation layer, not in the subsidiaries' local books.
  • After elimination, data is in Eliminated state — combined external activity, ready for ownership math.
