Layer 1 — Lesson 5

Key outputs

The output of consolidation is a set of financial statements for the group. Four main statements, plus supporting schedules. This lesson covers each one and explains what makes the consolidated version different from a single-company version.

The four main statements

1. Consolidated balance sheet

A snapshot of what the group owns and owes at a point in time — usually the last day of the fiscal period.

The structure looks like any company's balance sheet:

  • Assets — current and non-current
  • Liabilities — current and non-current
  • Equity

What's different in the consolidated version:

  • The numbers represent the whole group — combined cash from every subsidiary, combined receivables, combined property, etc.
  • Intercompany balances are gone. If Sub A owes Sub B $100, neither the receivable nor the payable appears in the consolidated balance sheet. They've been eliminated.
  • The parent's "Investment in Subsidiaries" line is gone. It was eliminated against the subsidiaries' equity.
  • Equity has a non-controlling interest line — the portion of subsidiary equity belonging to outside shareholders.
  • Goodwill may appear as an asset — from past acquisitions where the parent paid more than book value.
  • A CTA line sits within equity if there are foreign subsidiaries.

2. Consolidated income statement

The group's revenue, expenses, and resulting profit over the period. Same structure as any income statement:

  • Revenue
  • Cost of goods sold
  • Gross profit
  • Operating expenses
  • Operating income
  • Other income/expense, interest, taxes
  • Net income

What's different in the consolidated version:

  • Revenue is the group's external revenue only. Sales between subsidiaries are eliminated. So if Sub A sells $1M to Sub B and Sub B re-sells to external customers for $1.2M, the consolidated revenue is $1.2M, not $2.2M.
  • Expenses are similarly external only. Intercompany expenses are eliminated against the matching intercompany revenue.
  • Unrealized profit from intercompany sales is removed. If Sub A made a profit selling to Sub B and Sub B still has the goods in inventory, that profit is reversed.
  • The bottom of the statement is split. Net income is shown as "Net income attributable to parent shareholders" plus "Net income attributable to non-controlling interest."

3. Consolidated cash flow statement

Where the group's cash came from and went to during the period. Standard cash flow statement structure:

  • Operating activities
  • Investing activities
  • Financing activities

What's different in the consolidated version:

  • It can't just be the sum of subsidiary cash flows. Intercompany cash movements (one subsidiary paying another) net to zero at the group level — but each subsidiary's local cash flow statement shows them as outflows or inflows.
  • FX rate effects need their own line. Translating closing balances at a different rate than opening balances creates apparent "cash movements" that aren't real cash movements — they're just rate changes. These get pulled out into a separate "Effect of exchange rates on cash" line.
  • Acquisitions and disposals are presented at the group level. If the group bought a new subsidiary mid-year, the cash paid for the acquisition shows up in investing activities, and from the acquisition date forward, the acquired entity's cash flows are included in the group totals.

Cash flow consolidation is genuinely complex — more than just summing rows. It gets a full lesson in Layer 2.

4. Consolidated statement of changes in equity (equity rollforward)

A statement showing how the equity section moved from the beginning of the period to the end. It accounts for every change:

  • Opening equity balance
  • Plus net income for the period
  • Plus other comprehensive income (including CTA)
  • Less dividends paid
  • Plus/minus share issuances, buybacks, etc.
  • Plus/minus NCI movements (e.g., NCI's share of net income, NCI's share of dividends, changes in NCI from buying/selling shares)
  • Equals closing equity balance

This statement is what ties the period's income statement to the period's balance sheet — it shows how the period's profit became part of retained earnings, and how all other equity movements happened.

Supporting reports

In addition to the four main statements, consolidation typically produces several supporting outputs that are essential for management and for auditors:

Trial balance

A consolidated trial balance — every group account with its consolidated balance, showing debits equal credits. This is the raw input from which the main statements are built.

Intercompany matrix

A grid showing, for each pair of group entities, the intercompany balances between them — and whether the two sides agreed. Mismatches need investigation.

Flux / variance analysis

Comparison of current period to a prior period or to budget, showing the change ("flux") for each line item, often with explanations for material variances. This is what management uses to understand performance.

Disclosure notes

Financial statements come with extensive notes describing accounting policies, breakdowns of major line items, related party transactions, contingencies, and many other items. Some notes are produced automatically from the consolidation data; others are drafted by humans and cross-checked against the numbers.

The audit trail behind everything

Behind every number in every output, the system has to preserve a complete audit trail:

  • Which subsidiaries' submissions contributed to this line?
  • Which mappings were applied?
  • Which exchange rates were used, on which dates?
  • Which elimination entries were posted?
  • Which topside entries were posted by which person, when, and why?

An auditor's standard test is to pick a line in the consolidated statements and ask "show me where every dollar of this comes from." If the system can answer that question in a few clicks, the audit goes smoothly. If it can't, the audit gets long and expensive.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

The reporting layer of the application has to produce all of these outputs cleanly, but more importantly, every number it produces must be drillable — clicking should reveal the next layer of detail behind it. Drill from a consolidated statement line all the way down to the originating subsidiary trial balance entry. That's the architectural requirement that flows from the audit trail.

Output formats also matter. Many customers will export to Excel, PDF, or external reporting tools. Numbers must be consistent across formats.

Summary

  • Four main consolidated statements: balance sheet, income statement, cash flow, statement of changes in equity.
  • Each looks like a single-company statement but represents the whole group, with internal transactions removed and ownership math applied.
  • Supporting reports include the consolidated trial balance, intercompany matrix, flux analysis, and disclosure notes.
  • Behind every number is an audit trail — every consolidated figure should be traceable back to its source data.
