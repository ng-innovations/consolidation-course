Layer 1 — Lesson 6

Why Excel breaks at scale

Many companies start out doing consolidation in Excel. For a small group — say, two or three subsidiaries, one currency, simple ownership — Excel works fine. It's flexible, everyone knows how to use it, and the formulas are easy to write.

But as the group grows, Excel-based consolidation starts to break in predictable ways. Every consolidation software product on the market exists because of these breaking points. Understanding them is understanding what the product we're building has to solve.

How a typical Excel consolidation grows

Year 1: one workbook. A tab per subsidiary, a mapping tab, an eliminations tab, an output tab. A finance person spends two days each month assembling it.

Year 3: the company acquired three more subsidiaries, opened a European office, and entered a joint venture. Now the workbook has 30 tabs. The two-day close has become a five-day close. One spreadsheet wizard knows where all the formulas point.

Year 5: the company has 25 subsidiaries across 12 countries. There are now several workbooks linked together. The close takes 10 working days. The spreadsheet wizard has left the company. The new controller spends evenings tracing why one number changed.

This is the trajectory consolidation software is designed to interrupt.

Where Excel breaks

1. Formulas silently break

Excel formulas reference cells. When someone inserts a row, deletes a column, sorts a sheet, or renames a tab, formulas can break — sometimes loudly (with a #REF! error), sometimes silently (the formula still calculates, but on the wrong cells).

Silent breakage is the dangerous kind. The consolidated numbers might be off by thousands or millions, and no one notices until the auditor finds it.

2. No audit trail

Excel records the current state of every cell — but not who changed it, when, or what it used to be. If someone overrode a number on Tuesday and then changed it again on Thursday, there's no trace.

The closest Excel gets to an audit trail is "track changes" — but it's not designed for the volume or complexity of consolidation, and it can be turned off.

For a public company subject to SEC reporting, this is a serious compliance gap. Sarbanes-Oxley requires demonstrable internal controls over financial reporting. "It's in the spreadsheet" is not a control.

3. No real version control

Period N's close was based on a spreadsheet that has since been modified for Period N+1. The original numbers can still be reconstructed from the filed financial statements, but the working file that produced those numbers might no longer exist in its filed form.

If a prior period needs to be restated, or if an auditor asks to see the working papers that produced last year's numbers, recovering them from Excel files is often impossible.

4. No segregation of duties

Anyone with access to the file can change anything. There's no easy way to say "this person can submit subsidiary data, but only this other person can post topside entries." Internal controls become a matter of trust rather than enforcement.

5. Concurrency is brutal

Spreadsheets are single-user. When Sub A's controller and Sub B's controller both need to update their tabs at the same time, one of them is waiting. Modern shared spreadsheets (Google Sheets, shared Excel workbooks) help, but bring their own performance and locking issues at consolidation scale.

6. Intercompany matching is manual

Matching intercompany balances across subsidiaries is the most reliably painful part of every close. Sub A says it's owed $100 from Sub B. Sub B's books show $97. Someone has to figure out where the $3 went. In Excel, this means manually building matching grids each period, eyeballing differences, calling people, and rebuilding the grid when numbers change.

7. Currency translation gets convoluted

With one currency, translation isn't an issue. With three, it's tedious. With ten — closing rates, average rates, historical rates, different rate types per account, CTA calculations — the formulas become hairy enough that errors creep in.

8. No structured workflow

Did Sub A submit yet? Was Sub B's TB approved by the regional controller? Did the FX team post the latest rates? In Excel, this is tracked in email and Slack and someone's spreadsheet tracker. There's no system that knows the close is on day 4 of 10 and step 6 is blocked because Sub C hasn't submitted.

9. No drilldown

A consolidated number is the result of additions, eliminations, translations, and adjustments. In Excel, drilling from a consolidated total down to its source means tracing formulas by hand across tabs and files. It's slow, error-prone, and impossible at scale.

10. Performance degrades

A large consolidation workbook can take minutes to recalculate. When every change triggers a recalc, productivity craters. Workbooks over a few hundred MB become genuinely fragile — they corrupt, they fail to open, they lose formulas.

11. The knowledge lives in one person's head

The person who built the consolidation workbook understands how it works. Nobody else fully does. When that person leaves, retires, or even goes on vacation during the close, the company has a problem.

12. Errors are expensive

A 2009 study by Powell and Baker found that 88% of spreadsheets contained errors. For consolidation, errors are not just embarrassing — they can require restatement of public financial statements, trigger regulatory action, and damage management's credibility with investors.

When Excel is still fine

It's worth being honest: Excel is fine for small, simple consolidations. A two-entity group with one currency and no intercompany activity might never need a system. Many small businesses consolidate in Excel forever and have no problems.

The breaking point is when one or more of these is true:

  • 5+ subsidiaries
  • 2+ currencies
  • Material intercompany activity
  • Public reporting requirements
  • Acquisition or divestiture activity
  • More than one person involved in the close
  • A monthly close that takes more than a few days

Our product is for the companies past that line.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

This list is a useful design checklist. For every feature we build, we should ask: "How does this solve a problem that Excel users hit?" Features that don't trace back to a real pain point on this list might be solving the wrong thing.

Also worth noting: customers often arrive carrying Excel habits. Many will expect to be able to drop in a spreadsheet of overrides, build their own reports, and treat the consolidation engine as an "Excel that doesn't break." Some of those expectations are reasonable; some have to be carefully redirected toward the structured approach.

Summary

  • Excel works for small consolidations but breaks predictably as groups grow.
  • The breaking points include silent formula errors, no audit trail, no version control, no concurrency, manual intercompany matching, FX complexity, and concentration of knowledge.
  • Each weakness corresponds to a capability that consolidation software is designed to provide.
  • The product is for companies past the Excel breaking point — typically 5+ subsidiaries, multiple currencies, material intercompany activity, or public reporting.
