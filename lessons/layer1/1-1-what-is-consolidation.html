Layer 1 — Lesson 1

What is financial consolidation?

A large company is almost never one company. It is a parent at the top with a tree of subsidiaries beneath it — separate legal entities that exist for tax, regulatory, operational, or historical reasons.

Each subsidiary keeps its own books. Each has its own bank accounts, its own employees, its own customers and suppliers, and its own financial statements. From a legal standpoint, they are independent companies. The fact that the parent owns them doesn't change that.

But from a business standpoint, the parent and its subsidiaries function as one economic unit. They share strategy. They share capital. Profits flow up. When investors look at "the company," they don't want to see twelve separate balance sheets — they want one.

Financial consolidation is the process of combining the financial statements of a parent and its subsidiaries into a single set of statements that represents the whole group as one company.

The simple idea

Imagine the simplest possible group: a parent company P, and one wholly-owned subsidiary S. Each has its own books. To get the consolidated picture, you could try just adding their numbers together — line by line.

Sometimes this works. But not always — and the reasons it fails are exactly what makes consolidation a real subject rather than a spreadsheet exercise.

Why simple addition isn't enough

There are at least five reasons the naive "add it all up" approach falls apart:

1. Internal transactions inflate the numbers

If P sells $100 of services to S, P records $100 of revenue and S records $100 of expense. Both numbers are real on each company's local books. But the group hasn't actually earned anything from the outside world — it just moved money from one pocket to another. Adding them both into consolidated revenue would overstate what the group earned.

These intercompany transactions have to be removed before the group view is meaningful. That removal is called elimination.

2. The parent owns the subsidiary — but the subsidiary owns assets

On the parent's books, there's typically an asset called "Investment in Subsidiary" worth whatever P paid for S. On S's books, there are the actual underlying assets — the cash, the buildings, the inventory. You can't count both. From the group's perspective, the underlying assets are what's real; the investment line is just the parent's way of representing ownership. So the investment line is eliminated against the subsidiary's equity.

3. The parent doesn't always own 100%

If P only owns 80% of S, then 20% of S belongs to other shareholders. The consolidated statements still include 100% of S's assets and revenue (because P controls all of it) — but we have to separately track the portion that belongs to those other shareholders. That portion is called non-controlling interest.

4. Subsidiaries often use different currencies

A German subsidiary keeps its books in euros. A Japanese one in yen. You can't add euros and yen to dollars. Each subsidiary's numbers have to be translated into the group's reporting currency first — and the exchange rates used for different line items follow specific rules.

5. Subsidiaries may use different accounting policies and charts of accounts

One subsidiary might call something "Operating Cash"; another might call the same kind of thing "Bank — Main Account." One might depreciate buildings over 30 years; another over 40. Before anything can be combined, the data has to be standardized to the group's policies and chart of accounts.

What consolidation actually produces

The output of consolidation is the same set of financial statements that any company produces — except they describe the whole group:

  • A consolidated balance sheet
  • A consolidated income statement
  • A consolidated cash flow statement
  • A consolidated statement of changes in equity

These are the statements a public company files with regulators, sends to investors, and uses to manage the business. The consolidated view is the official view of the group's finances.

Why this matters for the software

For developers

Consolidation is not a single calculation — it's a pipeline. Raw subsidiary data goes in one end; consolidated statements come out the other. In between, there are mapping, validation, currency translation, standardization, elimination, ownership math, adjustments, and reporting — all in a specific order, all reversible, all auditable.

The application we're building is the engine that runs this pipeline. Every step needs to be deterministic, traceable, and auditable, because the numbers it produces are what a company shows to investors, regulators, and tax authorities.

Summary

  • Big companies are usually a parent plus many subsidiaries — separate legal entities, one economic unit.
  • Consolidation combines them into one set of group financial statements.
  • You can't just add the books together. Internal transactions, ownership, currency, and accounting differences all have to be reconciled.
  • The output is a standard set of financial statements describing the whole group.
