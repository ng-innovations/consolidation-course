Layer 1 — Lesson 3

The cast of characters

Consolidation has a specific vocabulary for the different relationships a company can have with another company. The terminology matters because each relationship gets a different accounting treatment.

The driving question is always: how much control does the investor have over the investee?

The control spectrum

Imagine a spectrum from "owns 0% — no relationship" up to "owns 100% — total ownership." Different points on the spectrum get different names and different accounting treatments.

The categories

Passive investment (typically under 20%)

The investor holds a small stake and has no influence on how the investee is run. Maybe the parent bought some shares of a publicly traded company as a financial investment.

Treatment: the investment is recorded at cost or fair value. The investee's books are not touched. If a dividend comes in, that's recorded as income; otherwise, the only change to the investment's value is from market price movements.

Associate — significant influence (typically 20% to 50%)

The investor has enough ownership to influence the investee but not control it. They might have a board seat, participate in policy decisions, or have a material voice in operations.

Such an investee is called an associate. Treatment: the equity method. The investor doesn't combine the investee's line items into its own statements; instead, it recognizes its share of the investee's profit each period through a single line.

Joint venture — joint control

A joint venture (JV) is a special case where two or more parties jointly control a business — neither has unilateral control. The classic setup is 50/50 ownership with shared decision rights.

Under modern IFRS and GAAP, joint ventures are accounted for using the equity method, just like associates.

Subsidiary — control (typically over 50%)

The investor has control — the power to direct the investee's activities. The investee is now a subsidiary of the investor.

Treatment: full consolidation. The subsidiary's assets, liabilities, revenue, and expenses are combined line-by-line into the parent's statements. The investment in the subsidiary is eliminated against the subsidiary's equity. Intercompany transactions are eliminated. If the parent doesn't own 100%, the rest is shown as non-controlling interest.

Control is about more than just the percentage

The ownership percentages above are useful guidelines, but the actual treatment depends on whether the investor has control, significant influence, or neither — and that determination can be more subtle than a simple count of shares.

For example, a company might own only 40% of another but still have control because:

  • It has contractual rights to appoint the majority of the board
  • The remaining shares are widely dispersed among small investors who never vote together
  • It has a call option that lets it buy more shares at any time

Conversely, a company might own 60% and not have control because of a shareholder agreement that requires unanimous consent for major decisions.

In edge cases — particularly with special-purpose vehicles — US GAAP uses the concept of a Variable Interest Entity (VIE): an entity controlled through contractual arrangements rather than equity ownership. If you're the "primary beneficiary" of a VIE, you must consolidate it as if it were a regular subsidiary, even with zero shares.

Non-controlling interest in more detail

When the parent owns most but not all of a subsidiary, the leftover portion belongs to other shareholders. This is the non-controlling interest (NCI), formerly called minority interest.

Here's the key insight: under full consolidation, we include 100% of the subsidiary's assets and revenue in the group statements — even though the parent only owns 80%. Why? Because the parent controls all of it. From an operational standpoint, the whole subsidiary belongs to the group's economic activity.

But we have to acknowledge that some of the resulting net worth and net income belongs to those other shareholders, not the parent's shareholders. That's what NCI does. It's a separate line in equity, and a separate line at the bottom of the income statement.

Indirect ownership

Most groups don't have a flat structure where the parent directly owns every subsidiary. Real-world groups look more like trees: the parent owns intermediate holding companies, which own operating subsidiaries, which sometimes own further subsidiaries.

In the diagram above:

  • P directly owns 100% of Holdco H and 80% of Sub C.
  • H owns 100% of Sub A and 70% of Sub B.
  • P's direct ownership of B is 0% — but P's indirect ownership through H is 100% × 70% = 70%.

For consolidation purposes, what matters is the effective ownership — the chain of percentages multiplied together. And whether the group must consolidate B depends on whether P, through H, controls it (which here, with 70%, it does).

Why this matters for the software

For developers

The entity model needs to support:

  • Multiple consolidation methods per entity (full, equity, fair value)
  • Ownership percentages that change over time (acquisitions, divestitures, step-ups)
  • Tree-structured ownership with indirect chains
  • The distinction between direct and effective ownership
  • NCI tracked as a first-class concept, not bolted on later

Most "gotchas" in consolidation systems come from oversimplifying the ownership model. It's worth getting this layer right early.

Summary

  • The accounting treatment of an investee depends on the degree of control the investor has.
  • Passive investments (typically <20%) — cost or fair value method.
  • Associates (20–50%, significant influence) — equity method.
  • Joint ventures — equity method under modern rules.
  • Subsidiaries (control, typically >50%) — full consolidation.
  • Real-world groups have tree structures with indirect ownership chains. Effective ownership is the product of the chain.
  • The portion of a subsidiary the parent doesn't own is called non-controlling interest.
