        <h1>Chart of accounts &amp; mapping</h1>
      </header>

<p>Before any data can flow through the consolidation pipeline, everyone has to agree on what the accounts <em>are</em>. Each subsidiary has its own <span class="term" data-term="coa">chart of accounts</span> reflecting how it runs its local books. The group needs one master chart that everything maps to.</p>

<p><strong>Pipeline reference:</strong> Mapping runs as part of ingest. After it applies, each subsidiary's trial balance is still in <span class="term" data-term="data-state-local">Local</span> state, but expressed in the group's account codes instead of the subsidiary's local codes.</p>

<h2>Why charts of accounts vary</h2>

<p>If every subsidiary was set up at the same time, by the same team, using the same software, they could all share one chart of accounts. That rarely happens. Subsidiaries acquire their charts for many reasons:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Local regulatory requirements.</strong> Many countries mandate specific chart-of-accounts structures (France, Spain, Germany, China all have prescribed national charts).</li>
  <li><strong>Different accounting software.</strong> Each system has its own default chart, and customizing it costs time.</li>
  <li><strong>History of acquisitions.</strong> A subsidiary that joined the group from a prior owner brings its existing chart with it.</li>
  <li><strong>Operational granularity.</strong> A retail sub might track inventory by product line; a services sub might track it by client. Their charts reflect what they actually need to manage.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is fine for local operations, but for the group view, one consistent set of accounts is needed.</p>

<h2>The structure of a group chart of accounts</h2>

<p>A group COA is usually organized hierarchically. The top level is the type of account; below that are progressively more specific categories.</p>

<p>Each account has properties beyond just its name and number:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Account type</strong> — asset, liability, equity, revenue, expense. Drives sign conventions and which financial statement it appears on.</li>
  <li><strong>FX rate type</strong> — for foreign subsidiaries, which exchange rate applies to this account (closing, average, historical).</li>
  <li><strong>Intercompany flag</strong> — does this account participate in intercompany matching?</li>
  <li><strong>Elimination behavior</strong> — does this account get eliminated? Against what?</li>
  <li><strong>Cash flow classification</strong> — operating, investing, or financing (for cash flow statement preparation).</li>
</ul>

<h2>The mapping</h2>

<p>A <span class="term" data-term="mapping">mapping</span> is the rule that says "this local account corresponds to this group account." Mappings are configured per subsidiary, since each one has its own local chart.</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Subsidiary</th><th>Local account</th><th>→</th><th>Group account</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub A (US)</td><td>1010 — Operating Cash</td><td>→</td><td>1110 — Cash &amp; equivalents</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub A (US)</td><td>1015 — Petty Cash</td><td>→</td><td>1110 — Cash &amp; equivalents</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub B (DE)</td><td>10100 — Kasse</td><td>→</td><td>1110 — Cash &amp; equivalents</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub B (DE)</td><td>10200 — Bankkonto</td><td>→</td><td>1110 — Cash &amp; equivalents</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub C (UK)</td><td>BANK-01</td><td>→</td><td>1110 — Cash &amp; equivalents</td></tr>
</table>

<p>The mapping above is many-to-one: many local accounts roll up to one group account. This is the most common case.</p>

<h3>Intercompany accounts get their own mapping</h3>

<p>The intercompany flag is one of the most important properties on a group account. The consolidation can't eliminate intercompany balances if it can't identify them. Where the subsidiary maintains separate accounts for intercompany versus external activity, those map to separate group accounts with the IC flag set:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Local</th><th>Group</th><th>IC flag</th></tr>
  <tr><td>1200 — AR external</td><td>1120 — Accounts Receivable</td><td>No</td></tr>
  <tr><td>1210 — AR intercompany</td><td>1121 — AR — Intercompany</td><td>Yes</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Not every subsidiary uses separate IC accounts &mdash; some encode the counterparty as a separate field on each line. Both encodings are handled at TB ingest; see <a href="2-2-trial-balance.html">Lesson 2.2</a>.</p>

<h2>Other dimensions that need mapping</h2>

<p>The chart of accounts is the main thing to map, but it's not the only one. Every <span class="term" data-term="segment">segment</span> on a transaction may need its own mapping:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Entity codes.</strong> Sub A's accounting system uses different entity codes than the group. The counterparty field on an intercompany transaction needs to be translated from local entity ID to group entity ID.</li>
  <li><strong>Department / cost center.</strong> If the group reports by function (Sales, R&amp;D, G&amp;A), each subsidiary's departments need to roll up.</li>
  <li><strong>Location.</strong> Local sites map to group regions.</li>
  <li><strong>Product line.</strong> If the group reports segment revenue by product, each subsidiary's product codes need to map.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Mapping maintenance</h2>

<p>Mappings aren't set-and-forget. Every time a subsidiary adds a new local account, somebody has to decide where it maps. If nobody decides, that account's data will either fail to load or load with no group account assigned.</p>

<h3>Common operational patterns</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Default mapping.</strong> New local accounts of a known pattern automatically map to a default group account.</li>
  <li><strong>Suspense / Unmapped.</strong> Accounts without a mapping route to a special "Unmapped" group account so they don't get lost &mdash; and so the validation phase can flag them for cleanup before the close completes.</li>
  <li><strong>Effective dates.</strong> Mappings change over time. The system needs to apply the mapping that was in effect for the period being consolidated, not the current mapping. Otherwise prior-period reports could change retroactively.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why this matters for the software</h2>

<div class="callout callout-dev">
  <div class="callout-title">For developers</div>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Mappings are versioned and effective-dated.</strong> Querying a mapping always requires "which period?"</li>
    <li><strong>Unmapped accounts shouldn't silently drop.</strong> Route them to a clearly-named Suspense bucket and surface them in validation reports.</li>
    <li><strong>The group COA includes more than account codes.</strong> Each account carries metadata (FX rate type, IC flag, cash flow classification) that drives downstream pipeline steps. Treat that metadata as a first-class part of the COA model.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<h2>Summary</h2>

<ul>
  <li>Subsidiaries have different local charts of accounts; the group needs one master chart, and a mapping per subsidiary that translates local accounts to group accounts.</li>
  <li>Group accounts carry metadata (type, FX rate, IC flag, elimination behavior, cash flow classification) that drives downstream pipeline steps.</li>
  <li>Other segments &mdash; entities, departments, locations, products &mdash; need mapping too.</li>
  <li>Mappings are versioned by period; unmapped accounts route to Suspense for cleanup before period close.</li>
</ul>

<p>Every consolidation cycle starts with the same task: collect a <span class="term" data-term="trial-balance">trial balance</span> from every subsidiary, and verify that what arrived is usable.</p>

<p><strong>Pipeline reference:</strong> Trial balance collection and validation runs at ingest. After it passes, the subsidiary's data lands in <span class="term" data-term="data-state-local">Local</span> state, ready for the rest of the pipeline.</p>

<h2>How TBs arrive</h2>

<p>Subsidiaries submit their TBs in different ways depending on the customer's setup:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Direct integration.</strong> The system pulls TBs from the subsidiary's accounting software automatically &mdash; NetSuite, SAP, Dynamics, QuickBooks, etc. The cleanest setup.</li>
  <li><strong>File upload.</strong> The subsidiary's controller exports a TB from their system to CSV or Excel and uploads it. The format usually has to match a defined template.</li>
  <li><strong>Web entry.</strong> For very small subsidiaries or non-financial entities, the data is sometimes keyed in directly.</li>
</ul>

<p>Whatever the source, what arrives is normalized into a common internal format before validation can run.</p>

<h2>The internal representation</h2>

<p>A typical internal TB line:</p>

<p>Storing the balance as a single signed amount (with credits negative) avoids carrying two columns where only one is ever populated per row. A trial balance "balances" when the signed amounts across all accounts sum to zero.</p>

<h2>How intercompany counterparty is identified</h2>

<p>Identifying the account, period, amount, and currency on a TB line is usually mechanical &mdash; that information falls out of the structure of the data itself. Identifying the intercompany counterparty is the part that varies, because subs encode it in their books in one of two ways. From the consolidation engine's perspective either is workable; the choice usually comes down to what each sub's source accounting system can produce.</p>

<h3>Approach A: account-encoded</h3>

<p>Have a separate account for each trading partner. The account code <em>is</em> the counterparty identifier. The engine can read the account and know who's on the other side.</p>

<h3>Approach B: dimensional</h3>

<p>Have one IC AR account and one IC <span class="term" data-term="ap">AP</span> account, and attach a "trading partner" tag (a dimension) to each line. The account stays single; the partner identification moves to a separate field on each line.</p>

<p>What drives which approach a sub uses is mostly the capability of its source accounting system. Smaller companies running simpler systems (QuickBooks and the like) tend toward Approach A &mdash; separate accounts &mdash; because that's what's natively exportable. Larger entities on full ERPs (NetSuite, SAP, Oracle, Workday) typically support dimensions natively and lean toward Approach B.</p>

<p>The ingest layer needs to accept either encoding and normalize them into a single internal representation, so downstream steps (matching, elimination) don't have to care which approach the sub used.</p>

<h2>The role of dimensions</h2>

<p>A trial balance line doesn't have to be one row per account. Modern systems usually capture additional <span class="term" data-term="segment">segments</span> on each row:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Entity</th><th>Account</th><th>Department</th><th>Counterparty</th><th class="num">Amount</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub A</td><td>4000 Revenue</td><td>Sales — North</td><td>(external)</td><td class="num">(300,000)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub A</td><td>4000 Revenue</td><td>Sales — South</td><td>(external)</td><td class="num">(100,000)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Sub A</td><td>4100 Rev — IC</td><td>Sales — North</td><td>Sub B</td><td class="num">(80,000)</td></tr>
</table>

<p>The dimensions become available for slicing the data in reports later. They also drive intercompany matching: the counterparty on Sub A's row tells the system "this $80,000 of revenue should match against Sub B's books."</p>

<h2>What validation checks</h2>

<p>Once a TB is in the system, validation has to verify it's usable. The checks fall into three buckets.</p>

<h3>Structural validation</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Every row has a valid account code (exists in the local <span class="term" data-term="coa">COA</span>).</li>
  <li>Every account code has a <span class="term" data-term="mapping">mapping</span> to a group account, or routes to Suspense.</li>
  <li>Required fields are present (amount, currency, period).</li>
  <li>Amounts are numeric and within sane bounds.</li>
  <li>The currency code matches the entity's expected <span class="term" data-term="functional-currency">functional currency</span>.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Mathematical validation</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The TB balances.</strong> Signed amounts sum to zero.</li>
  <li><strong>Opening balances tie to last period's closing balances.</strong> Each balance sheet account's opening balance should equal what the same account had at the prior period close. See <a href="2-3-carryforward.html">Lesson 2.3</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Sign conventions.</strong> Asset and expense accounts should have debit balances; liability, equity, and revenue accounts should have credit balances. Unusual signs should at least raise a warning.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Business validation</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Every expected subsidiary has submitted for the period.</li>
  <li>Materially unusual variances from prior period are flagged for review.</li>
  <li>Subsidiary-level intercompany totals agree to its counterparty's books within a tolerance.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What happens on validation failure</h2>

<p>The system decides what to do based on severity:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Severity</th><th>Behavior</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Critical (TB doesn't balance, no mapping at all)</td><td>Reject the submission. Block this entity from progressing.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Error (unmapped account, missing required field)</td><td>Accept but route problematic items to Suspense and surface them for resolution. Block period close until resolved.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Warning (unusual sign, large variance)</td><td>Accept and flag for review. Don't block close, but record acknowledgment.</td></tr>
</table>

<p>The principle: never let bad data flow silently through the pipeline. Every issue either gets resolved or gets explicitly acknowledged.</p>

        <h1>Carryforward &amp; opening balances</h1>
      </header>

<p>Financial periods don't exist in isolation. Each period's <span class="term" data-term="opening-balance">opening balance</span> is the prior period's closing balance, and the math has to work out at every transition. This lesson covers <span class="term" data-term="carryforward">carryforward</span> &mdash; the mechanics of rolling balances between periods.</p>

<p><strong>Pipeline reference:</strong> Carryforward shows up at both ends of the period cycle. At the start of a period, it validates that this period's opening balances match the prior period's closing. At the end (after lock), it rolls closing balances forward into the next period's opening, with extra work at fiscal year-end.</p>

<h2>Balance sheet vs. income statement behavior</h2>

<p>Carryforward works differently for the two main account types.</p>

<h3>Balance sheet accounts roll forward</h3>

<p>An <span class="term" data-term="assets">asset</span>, <span class="term" data-term="liabilities">liability</span>, or <span class="term" data-term="equity">equity</span> account's closing balance at the end of one period <em>is</em> the opening balance of the next period. The cash on hand on December 31 is the cash on hand on January 1.</p>

<p>This is true regardless of period frequency. Monthly closes work the same way &mdash; January closing cash becomes February opening cash.</p>

<h3>Income statement accounts reset to zero — but go somewhere first</h3>

<p>Revenue and expense accounts measure activity <em>during</em> a period. At the end of the fiscal year, they need to start fresh for the new year &mdash; January's revenue shouldn't include December's.</p>

<p>But the profit (or loss) for the year doesn't just disappear. It moves into <span class="term" data-term="retained-earnings">retained earnings</span> on the <span class="term" data-term="balance-sheet">balance sheet</span>. This is called the year-end closing entry.</p>

<p>At year end:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Revenue and expense accounts close to zero (and stay at zero on Jan 1 of the new year).</li>
  <li>Net income for the year is added to retained earnings (whose closing balance carries forward as the opening balance for the new year).</li>
</ul>

<h3>Mid-year monthly closes</h3>

<p>For monthly closes within a fiscal year, the income statement accounts <em>don't</em> reset. They accumulate year-to-date. At the end of Q1, the revenue account reads three months of revenue; at the end of Q2, six months.</p>

<h2>Why carryforward validation matters</h2>

<p>It seems obvious that this period's opening should match last period's closing. But in practice, it's frequently broken &mdash; and broken carryforward is one of the most common sources of consolidation errors.</p>

<h3>How it breaks</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Late entries.</strong> Someone posted an adjustment to last period after that period was closed (but before it was locked). The closing balance changed, but the opening of the new period wasn't updated.</li>
  <li><strong>Restatements.</strong> A prior period's numbers had to be corrected. The new opening balances need to reflect the correction.</li>
  <li><strong>Mapping changes.</strong> A local account that mapped to group account 1110 last period now maps to 1120. Without careful handling, the balance disappears from 1110 and appears in 1120 with no historical context.</li>
  <li><strong>Currency translation.</strong> A subsidiary's translated opening balance depends on the historical FX rate. If the rate used for translation has changed (or been corrected), opening balances move.</li>
  <li><strong>Acquisition/divestiture.</strong> A new subsidiary's opening balances need to be brought in. A divested one's need to be removed.</li>
</ul>

<h3>What the carryforward check does</h3>

<p>For every balance sheet account in every entity, the system compares:</p>

<table style="font-size: 13px;">
  <tr><th>Account</th><th class="num">Last period closing</th><th class="num">This period opening</th><th class="num">Diff</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Cash</td><td class="num">50,000</td><td class="num">50,000</td><td class="num">0 ✓</td></tr>
  <tr><td>AR</td><td class="num">85,000</td><td class="num">85,000</td><td class="num">0 ✓</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Inventory</td><td class="num">60,000</td><td class="num">62,000</td><td class="num" style="color:#dc2626">2,000 ✗</td></tr>
  <tr><td>AP</td><td class="num">40,000</td><td class="num">40,000</td><td class="num">0 ✓</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Any non-zero difference has to be investigated. Sometimes there's a legitimate reason (a posted prior-period adjustment); sometimes it's a real error to be fixed before the new period can proceed.</p>

<h2>Year-end carryforward steps</h2>

<p>At fiscal year-end, the carryforward operation has more work to do than a normal period transition:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Calculate net income.</strong> Sum the income statement accounts.</li>
  <li><strong>Post the closing entry.</strong> Move net income into retained earnings.</li>
  <li><strong>Reset income statement accounts to zero.</strong> They start fresh.</li>
  <li><strong>Carry forward balance sheet balances.</strong> Closing → next period's opening.</li>
  <li><strong>Carry forward equity components.</strong> CTA cumulative balance, other OCI components, accumulated NCI, etc.</li>
  <li><strong>Lock in beginning-of-year ownership percentages.</strong></li>
</ol>

<h2>Equity components carryforward</h2>

<p>Equity has multiple components, and each needs to be carried forward correctly:</p>

<table>
  <tr><th>Component</th><th>How it carries forward</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Share capital</td><td>Closing balance carries forward. Only changes via share issuance/buyback.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Retained earnings</td><td>Closing balance + net income for new period − dividends paid.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>CTA</td><td>Cumulative &mdash; carries forward. New period's translation adds to it.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>NCI</td><td>Closing balance + NCI's share of new period's income − NCI dividends.</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Other OCI</td><td>Cumulative &mdash; carries forward like CTA.</td></tr>
</table>

<div class="callout callout-dev">
  <div class="callout-title">For developers</div>
  <p>Carryforward is where periods touch each other.</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Locked periods must be immutable.</strong> Once Period N is locked, its closing balances cannot change &mdash; otherwise Period N+1's opening becomes a moving target. Authorized "unlock" should require explicit action and audit logging.</li>
    <li><strong>Opening balance is its own concept, not the same row as closing balance.</strong> Store and surface them separately. A common bug pattern: showing only "current period balance" makes it hard to debug carryforward issues.</li>
